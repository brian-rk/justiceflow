Dispatch No. 1198 | 1 July 2026 
Public confidence in Tanzania’s judicial system 
is strong, but gaps in access to justice remain 
Afrobarometer Dispatch No. 1198 | Jacqueline Mwaipopo and Brenda Mshumbushi 
 
Summary  
Access to justice for all is a cornerstone of democracy and good governance, empowering 
citizens to exercise their rights and hold leaders accountable (OECD, 2025). The Tanzanian 
government has affirmed its commitment to strengthening the rule of law and ensuring equal 
access to justice, in line with the United Nations’ (2015) Sustainable Development Goal 16 
(United Republic of Tanzania, 2019). 
In 2024, the government introduced a new criminal-justice policy aimed at strengthening 
justice delivery through enhanced coordination among criminal-justice institutions, expansion 
of the national prosecution office, integration of information and communication technology 
systems across justice-sector agencies, and greater application of the rule of law at a 
subnational level (Mosenda, 2024).  
Complementing these reforms, the judiciary has sought to expand access to judicial services 
across the country, reduce case backlogs, improve infrastructure, and increase public 
awareness of rights and legal-aid services. Recent initiatives include recruiting additional 
magistrates, expanding the network of primary courts at district and community levels, and 
promoting alternative dispute-resolution mechanisms to make justice more efficient and 
affordable (Moltmann-Willisch, 2025). Recent digitisation initiatives, including the automation 
of case-management systems, court proceedings, and translation services, are also intended 
to reduce delays and improve service delivery (Judiciary of Tanzania, 2023).  
Despite these initiatives, significant challenges remain. On the World Justice Project’s (2025) 
Rule of Law Index, Tanzania ranks 98th out of 143 countries, with comparatively stronger 
scores on order and security and on constraints on government powers, but lower 
performance on government openness and fundamental rights. Access to justice continues 
to be constrained by case backlogs, delays in court proceedings, and uneven access to 
judicial services. As of June 2024 the Court of Appeal alone had 5,900 pending cases, of 
which 1,248 had been unresolved for more than two years (Judiciary of Tanzania, 2024). 
This dispatch reports findings from a special survey module on access to justice included in 
Afrobarometer’s Round 10 surveys.  
Results show that while a majority of Tanzanians express confidence that ordinary people 
can obtain justice in the courts, significant minorities remain concerned about the 
affordability of taking a case to court, as well as the fairness and timeliness of judgements. 
Awareness of legal-aid services is also limited.  
Most Tanzanians trust the courts, though a majority perceive corruption among at least 
“some” judges and magistrates.  
Six in 10 citizens would rather turn to political leaders than to formal courts to resolve disputes. 
And substantial minorities express concerns about unequal treatment under the law, political 
influence in judicial decisions, and impunity for public officials who commit crimes.

Afrobarometer surveys 
Afrobarometer is a pan-African, nonpartisan survey research network that provides reliable 
data on African experiences and evaluations of democracy, governance, and quality of life. 
Ten survey rounds in up to 45 countries have been completed since 1999. Round 10 surveys 
(2024/2025) cover 38 countries. Afrobarometer’s National Partners conduct face-to-face 
interviews in the language of the respondent’s choice. 
The Afrobarometer team in Tanzania, led by REPOA, interviewed a nationally representative 
sample of 2,400 adult Tanzanians in June-July 2024. A sample of this size yields country-level 
results with a margin of error of +/-2 percentage points at a 95% confidence level. Previous 
surveys were conducted in Tanzania in 2001, 2003, 2005, 2008, 2012, 2014, 2017, 2021, and 
2022.

▪ Seven in 10 Tanzanians (71%) say they feel “somewhat confident” or “very confident” 
that ordinary people who are wronged can obtain justice in the courts,. 
▪ Majorities of citizens consider it “somewhat likely” or “very likely” that they could find 
legal advice (64%) and could afford to obtain it (56%).

and would obtain a fair resolution (56%), but fewer than half (47%) are confident 
that such a case would be resolved within a reasonable time. 
o Only about one in three respondents (35%) say they are aware of legal-aid 
services available in their community. 
▪ Six in 10 Tanzanians (59%) say they would turn to a political leader to help resolve a

▪ An overwhelming majority (84%) of citizens say they trust the courts “somewhat” or “a 
lot.”

Do the courts provide justice? 
In Tanzania, confidence in the justice system is relatively high: Seven in 10 (71%)1 say they are 
“somewhat confident” (43%) or “very confident” (27%) that ordinary people who are 
wronged can obtain justice in the courts (Figure 1). About one in four (27%) express little or no 
confidence.  
Figure 1: Can people obtain justice in the courts? | Tanzania | 2024  
 
Respondents were asked: In general, how confident are you that ordinary citizens who are wronged 
can obtain justice in the courts? 
 
More men (73%) than women (69%) expect that the courts will dispense justice (Figure 2).  
Faith in the courts rises as respondents’ educational status increases (from 64% among the 
unschooled to 79% among those with post-secondary education) and falls with greater 
material deprivation2 (from 77% among the well-off to 64% among the very poor).

Access to courts and legal services  
Affordability, fairness, and timeliness are essential dimensions of effective access to justice. 
When asked whether they could afford to take a case to court should the need arise, more 
than half (58%) of Tanzanians say it is “somewhat likely” or “very likely” that they would be 
able to, though 39% consider it unlikely (Figure 3). Similarly, 56% believe they could receive a 
fair resolution in court. Timeliness is a greater challenge: Fewer than half (47%) think cases are 
likely to be resolved within a reasonable period, compared to 45% who think this is unlikely.  
Men are slightly more confident than women that a court resolution would be fair (60% vs. 
53%) and timely (49% vs. 45%) (Figure 4). Belief in a fair and timely court case declines with 
age. 
Education and wealth are strongly associated with optimism: Tanzanians with post-secondary 
education are more likely than their less-educated peers to say a legal suit would likely be 
resolved fairly (81% vs. 49%-58%), as are the well-to-do compared to their poorer counterparts 
(72% vs. 47%-60%).  
Similar patterns apply to prospects of a timely resolution, albeit with smaller margins.

Ordinary people who want to take a case to court will likely require advice or support from 
lawyers or legal services. Do Tanzanians think they can identify and afford such support?  
Nearly two-thirds (64%) of citizens say it is “somewhat likely” or “very likely” that they could 
find needed legal support, while a slightly smaller majority (56%) believe they could afford it 
(Figure 5). But sizeable minorities are doubtful: 32% say they would struggle to find support, 
and 40% say they doubt they would be able to afford it. 
Figure 5: Ability to find and afford legal support | Tanzania | 2024 
 
Respondents were asked: If you had a legal problem such as a serious complaint against a business or 
member of your community, how likely do you think it is that: You could find the advice or support you 
need from lawyers or legal services to address or resolve the problem? You could afford to obtain the 
legal advice or support that you need? 
 
Financial barriers remain significant. About half of adults with no formal education say they 
would probably not be able to afford a court case (52%) or necessary legal support (49%), 
while nearly half of very poor respondents say the same (47% and 45%, respectively)  (Figure 
6). Both views decline with increases in educational attainment and living standards.  
All age groups are equally likely to say that it is “not very likely” or “not at all likely” that they 
would be able to pay for a court case (39%), but more senior citizens (47%) than younger 
Tanzanians (37%-41%) think legal support would be too expensive. 
Only about one-third (35%) of Tanzanians report knowing of legal-aid services in their 
community (Figure 7). Awareness is lowest among citizens with no formal education (22%), 
and rises to 50% among those with post-secondary schooling. 
More urbanites (40%) and men (39%) are familiar with legal-aid services than rural residents 
(32%) and women (32%).

Preferred avenues for resolving disputes  
Asked where they would go to resolve a legal problem such as a serious complaint against a 
business or a fellow member of their community, about six in 10 would approach political 
leaders (59%) (Figure 8).  
Tanzania is an outlier in this regard: Across 38 African countries surveyed in 2024/2025, 
Madagascar recorded the next-highest proportion of respondents who would turn to 
politicians for help with legal issues (29%), followed by Benin (22%) and Uganda (17%).   
Small minorities of Tanzanians would rather turn to the police (12%), government officials (9%), 
or a local court (5%), while even fewer would consult family members (3%), a lawyer (3%), or 
traditional leaders (2%). 
Figure 8: Where to go to resolve a legal problem | Tanzania | 2024 
 
Respondents were asked: If you had a legal problem such as a serious complaint against a business or 
member of your community, where would you be most likely to go to try to find a resolution? 
 
Looking at key demographic groups, majorities consistently prefer getting their legal 
problems resolved by political leaders (Figure 9). Rural residents are more likely than their 
urban counterparts to turn to them (64% vs. 53%), as are uneducated respondents 
compared to citizens with some level of formal education (69% vs. 56%-59%).  
Citizens with at least secondary schooling are more likely than their their less -educated peers 
to head to the police and less likely to approach a government official.  
Lived poverty has little bearing on respondents’ preferences.

Perceived corruption in the courts 
While public trust is high, there are concerns about judicial corruption. More than six in 10  
citizens (63%) believe that at least “some” judges and magistrates are corrupt, including 18% 
who think “most” or “all” of them are (Figure 13). Similar proportions say there are no corrupt 
judges (18%) or express uncertainty (19%). 
Perceptions of at least “some” judicial corruption rise to two-thirds or more among citizens 
with secondary (70%) or post-secondary (70%) education, men (68%), urban residents (68%), 
and respondents experiencing high lived poverty (67%) (Figure 14). 
Perceptions of widespread corruption are less prevalent than they were a decade ago: The 
share of Tanzanians who believe that “most” or “all” judges and magistrates are corrupt 
declined from 36% in 2014 to 18% in 2024 (Figure 15). But the share who think at least “some” 
judicial officers are involved in graft is up by 13 percentage points since 2021 (50%).

Independence and neutrality of the courts 
Tanzania’s Constitution establishes the independence of the judiciary. It also guarantees 
equality before the law, stating that “no person shall be discriminated against… on the basis 
of their nationality, tribe, place of origin, political opinion, colour, religion, sex or station in life”  
(United Republic of Tanzania, 1977). 
Tanzanians broadly perceive equal application of the law: Large majorities say that it is 
“rarely” or “never” the case that ordinary people who commit crimes go unpunished  (79%), 
that judges are influenced by powerful people when deciding cases (75%), and that people 
are treated unequally under the law (68%) (Figure 16). And while three in 10 respondents 
(30%) say officials who commit crimes “often” or “always go unpunished, 61% disagree.

Executive respect for the rule of law  
Tanzanians strongly support presidential respect for the rule of law: More than three-quarters 
agree that the president must obey the laws and courts even if she thinks they are wrong 
(76%) and that she must regularly report to Parliament about how taxpayers’ money is spent 
(80%) (Figure 18). And overwhelming majorities say President Samia Suluhu Hassan “rarely” or 
“never” ignores laws (82%) and Parliament (83%).

Views on the death penalty  
Although Tanzania retains the death penalty in law for certain serious offences, no 
executions have been carried out since 1994. Successive governments have maintained this 
practice, and many death sentences have been commuted to life imprisonment . 
Nonetheless, high courts continue to impose the death penalty to dozens of convicts each 
year (Wazambi, 2023). 
Citizens are tolerant of capital punishment: 59% say the death penalty is justified for the most 
serious crimes (Figure 19). But 39% say it is never justified.

Conclusion  
Afrobarometer survey data show that although Tanzania’s judicial system enjoys widespread 
trust, it still faces key challenges. A majority of citizens believe that at least some judges and 
magistrates are involved in corruption, and substantial minorities express doubts about their 
ability to afford to take a case to court. Meanwhile, only about one in three respondents are 
aware of legal-aid services in their community.  
Roughly three in 10 worry that officials who commit crimes go unpunished and that people 
are treated unequally under the law. And while many citizens believe they could obtain 
necessary legal support and receive a fair outcome if they went to court, fewer than half are 
confident that a case would be resolved in a timely fashion.

Strong levels of trust in courts, confidence in the judiciary’s ability to dispense justice in cases 
of wrongdoing, and a general perception that the president respects other branches of 
government provide a solid foundation for access to justice and respect for the rule of law. 
Addressing delays, expanding access to affordable legal aid, and clamping down on 
corruption in the judicial system could make justice more inclusive and further strengthen 
public confidence.
